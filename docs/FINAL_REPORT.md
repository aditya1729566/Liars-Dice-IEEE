# BLUFF.EXE evidence report

## Working game

The active ruleset is `fixed5_score5_quantity_only_v1`. Both players keep five dice, a raise must strictly increase quantity, every challenge awards exactly one point, and the first to five wins. The C++20 engine alone rolls dice, validates actions, and resolves outcomes. The loopback API persists private state, returns redacted views, records invalid attempts, and enforces versioned idempotent requests. The prior dice-elimination and lexicographic-bid runs and N=1 policy use different rules; they are excluded from all current-rule claims.

The supplied game-logic attachment ends midway through section 14. Sections 0–13, which fully define the playable rules and state transitions, were implemented; no claims are made about the missing tail.

A real browser completed a five-round match against the production C++ backend, including public bids, a reveal, explicit Next Round, and a 0–5 final score. A later production-browser check selected a trained candidate, saw its public opening bid, and verified that the AI dice remained concealed. The interface was subsequently redesigned as a 2D scorecard with CSS pip dice, crisp bidding controls, and a completed-match Research journal. The Research view displays recorded AI decisions and policy probabilities only after a match ends; active game views redact its dice and trace. Desktop, mobile, final-score, and Research screenshots are tracked in `docs/screenshots/` and copied to `outputs/`. No paid service was used.

## Agents and training

The five selectable agents are random, probability, aggressive, the wider sampled-CFR Apprentice, and adaptive. The probability heuristic computes an exact binomial prior for the five unseen dice; it does not infer a strategic posterior. The original sampled-CFR candidate was trained on the current fixed-five ruleset for 100,000 external-sampling iterations (seed 1729). The run completed in 14.33 seconds with 3,249 visited information sets and saved a checkpoint, manifest, and policy with SHA-256 `9054a212e2865189ee9358e344e3fba48298408edb96fbf8cc343e75d005b977`.

A second 100,000-iteration resource run with the same seed reproduced the exact strategy table (the JSON file hash differs only because its run ID differs). It took 14.53 seconds, about 6,882 iterations/second, on one CPU worker; `/usr/bin/time -l` measured 34,996,224 bytes maximum resident set size and zero swaps. The run's timing log and checkpoint are preserved separately. The original run's peak memory was not measured.

This policy uses an **imperfect-recall, one-round action abstraction**: an opening claim at quantity 1, then quantity +1 with the current or private modal face, or challenge. It falls back to the probability heuristic when an information set or legal action is unavailable. It is neither an exact solution nor a claim of full-match Nash play. The adaptive candidate uses only public past bids and revealed outcomes to adjust a bounded fraction of its decisions; it falls back to the frozen sampled policy. It has not been separately trained.

As a separate solver check, the quantity-only **one-die toy** enumerated all 36 deals and full histories. After 2,000 tabular CFR iterations, an independent information-set-respecting best response measured NashConv 0.0017316113 (sum of the two best-response utilities). The measured curve fell from 0.3463 at 10 iterations to 0.0017 at 2,000; see `docs/plots/quantity-n1-convergence.svg` and its run IDs in `docs/quantity-n1-curve.json`. This is an exact measurement for the toy, not an exploitability bound for the fixed-five game.

## Measured evaluations

Each matchup was run in both seat orders with deterministic held-out seeds. Policy and engine SHA-256 values are frozen in the manifests. Wilson intervals are 95%; latency includes a fresh engine CLI process. No sampled agent made an illegal move in the completed runs or tests.

The initial three-agent baseline favored probability: it beat aggressive 980/1,000 as Human and 977/1,000 as AI, while random beat probability 0/1,000 in either seat. Baseline mean decision latency was 2.147 ms and p95 was 2.395 ms.

The five-agent screen measured CFR beating probability **308/400 (77.0%, Wilson 72.6–80.9%)**. In the separate hard-opponent run, CFR beat probability **301/400 (75.3%, 70.8–79.2%)**, conservative **293/400 (73.3%, 68.7–77.4%)**, high-call **322/400 (80.5%, 76.3–84.1%)**, and always-call **391/400 (97.8%, 95.8–98.8%)**. These CFR pairings remain valid because the frozen CFR action code is unchanged. Adaptive rows in both historical runs are retained in the raw records but **not used for promotion**: the seat-swapped adaptive model counted its own revealed bids in the Human seat, and its original threshold adjustment had no effect at the discrete five-dice prior values. The adaptive implementation was corrected and evaluated in `fixed5_adaptive_corrected_400`.

The hard run recorded 24,076 trained-policy hits for CFR out of 24,085 CFR decisions; the remainder used fallback. CFR mean decision latency was 4.799 ms, p95 5.312 ms, maximum 23.853 ms. These are local batch measurements, not a prolonged kiosk-load benchmark. The screening and hard-opponent runs are descriptive; they have 200 matches per seat and are insufficient by themselves for the preregistered champion promotion.

The 1,000-per-seat paired-seed confirmation measured original CFR beating probability **1,546/2,000 (77.3%, Wilson 75.4–79.1%)**, conservative **1,546/2,000 (77.3%, 75.4–79.1%)**, and high-call **1,651/2,000 (82.6%, 80.8–84.2%)**. Across the conservative and high-call league, original CFR's win rate exceeded probability's by **7.15 percentage points**, with a 20,000-resample paired bootstrap 95% interval **[5.73, 8.55]** percentage points. Each seed block preserved both seats and both opponent results. Original CFR had 74,439 policy hits in 74,455 decisions; p95 CLI latency was 6.024 ms and maximum 38.290 ms. Its direct and opponent-specific results, zero illegal moves, and latency passed the registered promotion gates. It was the first measured champion for this local opponent pool; this does not establish global optimality.

The conservative and probability heuristics made identical choices in this mode because their different call thresholds fall between the same discrete five-unknown-dice prior values. Their direct matchup was 1,000/2,000 for the first-named agent, and their results against CFR matched. This reduces the effective diversity of the confirmatory pool; high-call and the earlier random/aggressive/always-call opponents provide the distinct robustness checks. No policy parameters were changed after the screen.

The corrected adaptive agent won **372/800 versus frozen CFR (46.5%, Wilson 43.1–50.0%)**, **588/800 versus probability (73.5%, 70.3–76.4%)**, **657/800 versus high-call (82.1%, 79.3–84.6%)**, and **695/800 versus the round-three strategy switcher (86.9%, 84.4–89.0%)**. It did not meet the registered direct-CFR promotion gate, so it remains experimental. The public-outcome audit found 1,845/4,000 matches reached three confirmed opponent-final-bid reveals; 11,489/51,960 adaptive decisions occurred after that threshold. Its mean first eligible round was 5.21. These are opportunities to adapt, not a count of sampled exploitative branches. Adaptive p95 CLI decision latency was 8.493 ms, with a 49.784 ms maximum.

Fresh-seed paired controls measured adaptive **+4.94 percentage points versus probability** across conservative and high-call (95% seed-block bootstrap **[+2.75, +7.19]**), **+14.5 points versus probability** against the round-three switcher (**[+11.25, +17.75]**), and **−2.0 points versus frozen CFR** against the switcher (**[−4.38, +0.25]**). The switcher controls separately recorded CFR winning 711/800 and probability 579/800. These comparisons show adaptation's performance in this limited opponent pool; they do not override its failed direct-CFR gate or establish benefit against human strategy shifts.

## Wider CFR and the redesigned table, 8 November

The replacement Apprentice trained on the same fixed-five rules for **100,000 external-sampling iterations** (seed 20261108). It retains one-round point utility and imperfect recall, but adds quantity-two openings, one- or two-step raises with the current or private modal face, and the previous public bid in its information key. Training took **44.21 seconds** and produced **24,599** policy information sets, a resumable checkpoint, and policy SHA-256 `d99c9c002989c65fed62348b8ea623974436b7ebc75f9a0821c6e5edee99d9a8`. A separate fresh-process decision measured **0.04 seconds** elapsed and **14,876,672 bytes** maximum resident set size with zero swaps. This policy still omits larger jumps, has no full-game equilibrium guarantee, and has no measured fixed-five exploitability bound.

The follow-up promotion rule was recorded before the 2,400-match confirmatory run: v2 must beat original CFR directly with Wilson 95% lower bound above 50%, exceed a 60% lower bound versus probability, have zero illegal moves or errors, and keep fresh-process decision p95 under 100 ms. With paired seeds and both seats, v2 won **523/800 versus original CFR (65.4%, Wilson 62.0–68.6%)** and **594/800 versus probability (74.3%, 71.1–77.2%)**. The run logged **69,298 decisions**, zero errors, **21,154 policy hits in 21,190 v2 decisions**, and **40.25 ms p95** v2 CLI latency (maximum **145.30 ms**). All registered gates passed, so `cfr2` is the active selectable Apprentice in `policies/champions.json`.

The original CFR won **613/800 versus probability (76.6%, Wilson 73.6–79.4%)** on those same seeds. The paired v2-minus-original difference was **−2.375 percentage points**, with a 20,000-resample seed-block 95% interval **[−6.125, +1.5]** points. V2 therefore did not show an improvement against that opponent. An exploratory truthful-bid probe, designed after inspecting local human play and excluded from the promotion gate, found v2 **229/400 (57.3%)** and original CFR **157/400 (39.3%)** against the probe; the paired difference was **+18 points [12.5, 23.5]**. This supports a narrower claim that v2 is less vulnerable to that specific truthful-bid pattern. It cannot establish that human players will not exploit it. The one-die toy NashConv does not transfer to this five-dice abstraction.

The interface now presents a flat two-dimensional dice table, a visible score to five, explicit legal controls, and clear round reveals. After every completed human-versus-AI match, the Research tab can display the persisted decision trace for that browser session: the AI's own hand at each move, the public claim it faced, its chosen action, binomial truth prior, the action's sampled-policy weight and top sampled alternatives when applicable, and the later revealed outcome. Those numeric quantities describe recorded inputs and stochastic policy choices, not a human-readable internal chain of thought or a calibrated chance of winning. The API withholds the private trace until the final score. Older completed matches can replay authoritative events, while missing AI decision sources are labeled unavailable rather than invented. The page also displays tracked evaluation summaries with seeds and checksums.

## Complete match replay, 8 November follow-up

The Research page now presents a discrete slide for each round start, bid, challenge, reveal, next-round transition, and match end. The server reconstructs these from the persisted authoritative event log and joins each completed round's revealed hands with the recorded AI decision source, truth prior, sampled policy weight, and top policy options. A challenge slide is inserted immediately before its reveal because the engine stores the call as part of the reveal event. Both hands and the actual face count are clearly marked as **post-match evidence**; they were not visible to the AI during play.

For a human bid or challenge, the replay supplies a first-person **AI read** based on the public action and the AI's own five dice. It mentions the quantity jump, face change, fair-dice truth prior, and plausible interpretations. This text is a deterministic retrospective hypothesis, **not** a recovered CFR thought, a strategic posterior, or knowledge of the human's motive. For AI actions with a recorded trace, the slide reports the actual action source and sampled policy weight; older actions without a trace say so. The replay remains HTTP 409 until the match ends.

A production-browser check opened a five-round, 26-slide match, moved between human and AI actions, jumped to round five and the final score, and advanced with the keyboard ArrowRight key. The sampled AI raise showed its recorded **73.1%** policy weight and **59.8%** fair-dice truth prior. The mobile replay was checked at a 390-pixel viewport with a 375-pixel document width and no horizontal overflow after a grid fix. A local integrity audit generated **1,569 slides across 38 stored completed matches**, checking event coverage, one challenge per round, and the final slide. These stored matches include development checks, so this audit is not a human-performance result. Screenshots: `docs/screenshots/bluff-replay-human.png`, `bluff-replay-ai.png`, and `bluff-replay-mobile.png`.

## Verification and limits

`make verify` passed on the final implementation: C++ build, **10 engine tests, 4 trainer tests, 4 API tests**, ESLint, TypeScript, and the Next.js production build. `make kiosk` then built and served the production frontend and C++ engine. Engine tests cover quantity-only raises, forced call at quantity 10, random opener, fixed dice, scoring, redaction, replay, both trained agents' legal moves and hidden-human-dice invariance, and decision-trace privacy. API tests cover invalid attempts including malformed JSON, stale versions, duplicate requests, explicit Next Round, deletion, five-agent metadata, trained-agent creation, and the completed-only research endpoint. The production browser completed a five-round match against v2 CFR, verified five concealed AI dice during bidding, then found five decision records after the 0–5 final. A second browser match produced a sampled-CFR move with a **59.8% binomial truth prior**, **73.1% sampled action weight**, and the top three policy options in its journal. Desktop and 390-pixel mobile views were inspected; the mobile document width was **375 pixels** inside a **390-pixel** viewport, with no horizontal overflow. Prolonged kiosk load and fixed-five exact or restricted best-response/exploitability calculations remain unrun. The toy calculation does not validate five-dice equilibrium play.

Training, checkpoints, manifests, raw evaluation databases, summaries, and plots are preserved in deliverable archives. The v2 training, screen, truthful probe, and confirmatory archives passed ZIP CRC checks; their summaries and comparison are also tracked under `docs/`. The evaluator enforced a 1.5 GiB artifact cap and 2 GiB free-disk guard; the trainer uses one CPU worker and the same guards. The confirmatory evaluator process checked the original CFR policy hash at runtime; both tracked policy hashes were appended to its manifest after completion from unchanged policy files, with that provenance stated in the manifest.

## Staff explanation

Visitors see their five dice and claim how many of all ten dice show one face. They can raise the number or challenge the AI's claim. The AI sees only its own five dice and public moves, so it can be wrong or bluff. A challenge reveals both hands and gives one point to the round winner. First to five points wins. The Calculator uses exact odds for unseen dice; the Apprentice and Observer use an approximate trained policy, with the Observer adjusting from public past outcomes. The exhibition does not claim these policies solve the full game.
